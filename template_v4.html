How to use this map

Getting around

Drag to pan, scroll to zoom, or use the + and − buttons on the map. The target button under them returns to the opening view of central Bengaluru.

The chip at the bottom left of the map shows the scale and the latitude and longitude under your pointer. When the pointer is off the map it shows the centre of the current view.

What the map shows

The chips under the title pick what is drawn: All, Lakes today, Lost lakes or the primary Drains. Ward outlines stay on.

Hover over an existing or lost water body to see its name and area; click it for full details. Lost lakes in the menu lists every lost water body; pick one to fly to it.

The Layers panel at the top left of the map has every layer. The buttons at the top switch the basemap between the Esri street Map and Satellite imagery. Each layer has a checkbox to show or hide it and a colour key drawn in its own colour and line thickness. Secondary drains and Corporations start switched off. Press the panel's title to fold it away.

Citizen audits in the menu switches on the points recorded by volunteers in MOD Foundation's citizen audits of stormwater drains in 2026. Only submissions the team validated are shown. In the Layers panel, the menu under each audit layer picks what the dot colours show, and the key counts the points for each answer. Click a dot for everything recorded there.

My nearest lake asks your browser for your location, finds the closest existing lake and flies there. Your location is not stored or sent anywhere.

Finding a ward

Click on a ward directly on the map to pick it. You can also type at least two characters in the search box to match ward names, then click a result to fly to it.

Clicking a drain shows its popup without changing what you have selected, because a report covers a ward or a water body and a drain often runs through several wards.

Reset under the report button clears the current pick and empties the search box. It leaves the map where it is and the chips as you set them.

Generating a report

Pick a ward, by clicking it or by search, then press Generate report. A new window opens with the details, a small key map showing where the ward sits in the city, a map of the ward, tables of the existing and lost water bodies in that ward and a list of the drains crossing it. Use Print / Save as PDF in that window to keep it.

The drain list gives the length of each drain inside the ward, measured from the mapped lines. A drain with under 20 m inside the ward only touches its edge and is left out. Drains with no recorded name are given as a total length rather than listed.

A short summary at the top gives the ward's headline facts and compares it with the rest of the city: its lost water area is ranked only among wards that lost any, and its drains are compared per sq km so bigger wards do not always come first. Citizen audits are not ranked, as a ward without audits has not been checked. The report ends with next steps for residents and officials.

Where citizen audits found a problem (black water, a strong sewage smell, unauthorised inlets, garbage, stagnant water or a damaged retaining wall), an appendix lists each of those sites with its drain, side, date and location, linked to Google Maps. The sites are numbered on the ward map. A closing note, About this data, gives the sources, how things were measured and what the data cannot show.

Where citizens have audited drains in the ward, the report also shows what they found (water colour, smell, garbage, unauthorised inlets, retaining walls) with a short note on what each answer usually means, and marks the audit points on its map.

The tables give the area of each water body that lies inside the ward. A water body crossing a ward boundary is split between the wards, and pieces under 0.1 acres are left out.

The report draws its own map, so it does not matter what the screen is showing when you press the button. It always frames the whole ward with every layer switched on, and outlines the subject in blue.

A report about a water body frames that water body's ward too, so the map and the tables beneath it describe the same place. The tables likewise always list every water body recorded in the ward, whatever the layer switches say, so a report is never missing part of the record.

The report map is drawn as vector artwork from the data itself, so it appears at once and has no basemap photograph underneath. Neighbouring wards, the water bodies and the drain network give it its context, and it stays sharp however far you zoom into the PDF.

Asking questions

Press Ask about your ward at the top of the map and type a question, such as Has Mylasandra lost any lakes? or Drains in Kengeri. You can name the ward, or a lake in it, and it remembers the ward for your next question; Change ward starts again. The buttons under the box ask the usual questions for you.

It is not an AI. It looks for a ward or lake name and a topic (lost lakes, lakes left, drains, citizen audits, or how the ward compares with the city) and answers from the same data as the report, so the two always agree. If it cannot find a ward or a topic, it says so rather than guessing. Every answer links to the ward on the map and to its full report.

Reporting on your own area

To report on an area that is not a ward, such as a neighbourhood, a residents' association boundary or a project site, press Or upload your area (KML) under the report button and choose a KML file with the area drawn as a polygon (in Google Earth, draw a polygon and use Save Place As, then choose KML). The map zooms to the area; then press Generate report.

The report lists the water bodies, lost water bodies, drains and citizen audit points inside the area, which wards it covers, and the same summary, site list and notes as a ward report. It has no city ranking, as that only compares wards with each other. Only polygons in the file are used; points and lines are ignored, and zipped KMZ files are not read. The file is read in your browser and is never uploaded anywhere. Reset removes the area.

About this map

Why I built this

From March 2025 to February 2026 I worked with MOD Foundation on Building a Resilient Bengaluru. Volunteer teams walked the city’s stormwater drains and recorded what they found at each point: the water colour, the smell, any garbage, unauthorised inlets, broken retaining walls. By the end there was a large, careful dataset.

In May 2026 I went to a DataJam, and one thought stayed with me afterwards: if I were building something from this data for residents, what would it be? This map is my answer so far. It reflects my own understanding of what residents need, and it’s a work in progress.

What it does

  • Shows the city’s water on one map. Existing lakes, lost lakes, primary and secondary drains, and ward and corporation boundaries are separate layers you can switch on and off.
  • Ask about your ward. Type a ward name or a lake near you and ask a plain question, like “Has Mylasandra lost any lakes?” or “What did audits find?” The answers come from the map’s own data, not from AI. I added this because I expect people will want to ask rather than click through layers.
  • Gives every ward a full report. Each report covers its water bodies, the drains that cross it (with the length inside the ward), what citizen audits found and what each finding usually means, and next steps for residents and officials. It ends with a numbered list of sites that need follow-up, and each site links to its exact spot in Google Maps.
  • Lets you report on your own area. Upload a KML polygon of your neighbourhood or project site and get the same report for that area. The file is read in your browser and never uploaded.
  • Saves as a PDF, so you can share it with your ward office, ward committee or residents’ group.

About the data

  • Source: Building a Resilient Bengaluru, data accessed on 23 May 2026.
  • The year shown for a lost lake is the last survey it appears in. It is not the year the lake was lost.
  • Audit points are visual checks by trained volunteers, not lab tests. They show where to look more closely. Water quality can only be confirmed by testing, for example by the Karnataka State Pollution Control Board.
  • Some numbers are clipped to the boundary. Drain lengths and lake areas count only the part inside the ward or area. Drains with less than 20 m inside, and lake pieces under 0.1 acres, are left out.

What’s next

I’d like people to be able to talk to this map one day. A voice layer, perhaps through Wispr Flow, would let a resident ask about their ward out loud instead of typing. That matters in a city where many people are more comfortable speaking than typing, and in more than one language.
